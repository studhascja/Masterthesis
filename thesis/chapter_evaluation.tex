\chapter{Evaluation} \label{sec:eval}
In this chapter, the test methodology, the different test scenarios and their results are presented.  
Chapter \ref{subsec:procedure} discusses the boundary conditions of the test runs.  
Subsequently, in Chapters \ref{subsec:low}, \ref{subsec:medium} and \ref{subsec:high}, the procedure is applied under environments with varying levels of interference.  
Finally, Chapter \ref{subsec:disc} compares the results of this thesis with findings from related work and places them in context.  

\section{Test procedure} \label{subsec:procedure}

Certain factors remain constant across all test runs.  
The test procedure is defined by five key elements:  
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{cycle time}, which in this thesis is fixed at 3 ms as previously described.  
    \item The \textbf{packet size and bandwith of bulk data transmission}, which must be determined to minimize interference with the real-time application and simultaneously deliver a reasonable amount of data.  
    \item The \textbf{duration of a single test run}, i.e., the time during which the RT-application executes its computation.  
    \item The \textbf{number of repetitions} of each test run, i.e., how often a given test case is executed to produce sufficient data for reliable conclusions.  
    \item The \textbf{different test cases}, derived from the Wi-Fi factors outlined in Chapter \ref{sec:implementation}.  
    In this thesis, a total of 112 different test cases are considered.  
\end{itemize}  

To determine the execution time of the real-time application, both the number of repetitions and potential clock drift must be taken into account.  
In this thesis, each test case is executed for 60 seconds.  
As described in \cite[p. 3]{drift}, quartz clocks can exhibit a drift of 1-100 ppm (parts per million).  
This corresponds to a drift of 60 $\mu$s to 6 ms per minute.  
However, in the results of this thesis, no noticeable drift was observed within a one-minute interval.  

With a cycle time of 3 ms, each 60-second run consists of 20,000 cycles.  
Each test case is repeated three times, resulting in 60,000 cycles per case to increase the probability of capturing anomalies.  
Additionally, before each run, the system clock is synchronized, effectively eliminating clock drift.  
The Wi-Fi connection is also given a warm-up period of 60 seconds, as recommended in \cite[Chap.~3.3.3.14]{alexander-bmwg-wlan-switch-meth-01}, to avoid distortions in the results.  

Preliminary experiments were conducted to select the throughput and packet size.  
The results are presented in Table~\ref{tab:throughput} and Table~\ref{tab:packetsize}.  

For the tests regarding the impact of the bulk data volume, the \ac{UDP} protocol with the standard packet size of 1440~Bytes was used.  
The selection of the bulk data volume is challenging, as each standard has a different maximum data rate depending on modulation and available bandwidth.  
Therefore, the bulk data volume would ideally need to be chosen proportionally to the maximum data rate in each case.  

However, this work aims to investigate the limits of real-time capability of Wi-Fi when it simultaneously transmits a certain amount of additional data.  
For this reason, a fixed amount of bulk data is selected, which remains identical across all standards and bandwidths.  
Consequently, standards with higher maximum data rates experience a lower relative load from the bulk data and therefore benefit in terms of real-time performance.  
This decision is motivated by the fact that, in practice, the relative load caused by other data traffic is not always known, and thus a Wi-Fi system with higher capacity inherently provides an advantage.

Table~\ref{tab:throughput} presents the results of the preliminary experiments regarding the bulk data volume.  
The table shows the average and maximum latency\footnote{Latency is defined as the time required to complete a cycle of the real-time application.} of one cycle of the real-time application for data rates of 5, 20, 30 and 50~Mbit/s.  

It can be observed that, in particular, the maximum latency increases significantly with higher data rates.  
It is also evident that the maximum latency at 20~Mbit/s is higher than at 30~Mbit/s.  
This effect can be attributed to an unfavorable execution-time constellation in the test run at 20~Mbit/s.  
This explanation is supported by the average latency, which increases continuously with the data rate.  

The maximum data rate of Wi-Fi~4 with 20~MHz bandwidth is 76.9~Mbit/s.  
Thus, the results of the test case with 20~Mbit/s are still realistic, but for this study, a data rate of 30~Mbit/s is selected.  
The test runs with 5~Mbit/s show up to 45\% lower maximum latency in the low-bandwidth cases compared to the runs with 30~Mbit/s.  
However, in a network that carries both real-time and bulk data traffic, it is not realistic to limit the non-real-time traffic to 5~Mbit/s.  
At 30~Mbit/s, the maximum data rate of Wi-Fi~4 with 20~MHz bandwidth is utilized by 39\%, and the preliminary experiments did not show significant degradations compared to 20~Mbit/s.

These experiments were carried out using a data rate of 30~Mbit/s with the \ac{UDP} protocol.

The optimal packet size, as shown in green in Table \ref{tab:packetsize}, is 4096 bytes per packet.  
At this size, both latency and jitter are significantly lower than with 512-byte packets or the standard size of 1440 bytes.  
Furthermore, both latency values are lower than for larger packet sizes.  
However, it is noticeable that larger packet sizes perform better than smaller ones.  

Based on the results of the preliminary experiments, this study employs a data rate of 30~Mbit/s with a packet size of 4096~Bytes for the bulk data.  

The test cases are defined as combinations of Wi-Fi standard, frequency, bandwidth, \ac{QoS}, and transport protocol.  
For Wi-Fi~4, three combinations exist: the 2.4 GHz band and in the 5 GHz band both 20 MHz and 40 MHz channel widths.  
For Wi-Fi~5, the 5 GHz band with 20 MHz, 40 MHz and 80 MHz channel widths yields three combinations.  
Wi-Fi~6 supports all three frequency bands.  
In the 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands, four combinations arise, while in the 6 GHz band, the available channel widths of 20, 40, 80 and 160 MHz yield four more combinations.  
In total, Wi-Fi~6 therefore contributes eight combinations.  
Together, across all standards, 14 unique combinations of standards, frequency bands, and bandwidths are considered.  

Each of these 14 combinations is tested with four differnet levels of \ac{QoS} and with both \ac{TCP} and \ac{UDP}.  
This results in a total of $8 \times 14 = 112$ test cases.  

In the following, the test runs in an environment with low interference are discussed first.  

\section{Low disturbance test} \label{subsec:low}

In this test scenario, the experiments were conducted in an \ac{EMC} laboratory to bypass theoratical interference factors.  
The objective of these experiments is to evaluate the behavior of the different test cases in an ideal environment.  
The results can subsequently be used to assess the impact of real-world conditions on the Wi-Fi connection.  
In the \ac{EMC} laboratory, neither external electromagnetic interference nor additional influencing factors such as electrical devices or human presence are present.  

The results of the experiments are illustrated in Figures \ref{fig:tcp_low} and \ref{fig:udp_low}.  
A distinction is made between \ac{TCP}- and \ac{UDP}-based test cases.  
The naming convention of the test cases follows the pattern: Standard-Frequency-Bandwidth-QoS-Protocol.  
A value of “1” in the \ac{QoS} field indicates that \ac{QoS} is enabled.  
Specifically, the value 0xB8 was set as the \ac{DSCP} flag.  
This corresponds to the \ac{EF} class, which is intended for latency-critical applications, as described in the official \ac{IETF} document RFC 3246 \cite{rfc3246}.  
This class is particularly designed for voice and video applications that require low latency and minimal jitter.  

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{\input{figures/jitter_tcp_boxplot_3.pgf}}
    \caption{Presentation of the jitter of the TCP test cases in a low-interference environment.}
    \label{fig:tcp_low}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:tcp_low} presents the test cases using the \ac{TCP} protocol.  
It can be observed that test cases with enabled \ac{QoS} consistently exhibit significantly lower maximum values. 
An improvement of up to 70\% is achieved in some cases.
The mean values are also lower in most cases, although the difference is comparatively small.  
A similar behavior is observed in the \ac{UDP} test cases shown in Figure \ref{fig:udp_low}.  

Among the phases described in Section \ref{sec:implementation}, Phases 3 and 6 account for the largest share of the cycle time, together exceeding 90~\% in nearly all cases.  
These phases begin with the transfer of the packet to the driver queue via the Tracepoint \texttt{net\_dev\_xmit} and end with the confirmation of reception at the target device via the Tracepoint \texttt{netif\_receive\_skb}.  
A finer subdivision is not possible within this part of the network stack since no additional tracepoints exist.  
The significantly reduced jitter maxima in test cases with enabled \ac{QoS} affect only Phases 3 and 6.  
This is due to the preferential treatment of packets with the \ac{DSCP} flag set in both the driver and the firmware.  
As described in \cite{rfc3246}, the standard for the \ac{EF} class specifies shorter queues and reduced backoff times.  
Consequently, the delays within the driver and firmware are reduced, preventing occasional high latencies.  
However, this is not guaranteed, as illustrated by test case 6-5-80-1-TCP in Figure \ref{fig:tcp_low}, which shows comparatively high maximum values despite \ac{QoS} being enabled.  

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{\input{figures/jitter_udp_boxplot_3.pgf}}
    \caption{Presentation of the jitter of the UDP test cases in a low-interference environment.}
    \label{fig:udp_low}
\end{figure}

It is also noteworthy that the \ac{TCP} test cases consistently show lower mean jitter and mean latency compared to the \ac{UDP} test cases.  
This observation is unexpected, as the connection establishment and error correction mechanisms in \ac{TCP} would typically suggest lower performance.  
The maximum values, however, are approximately the same for both protocols.  

Another unexpected observation concerns the test cases with 80~MHz bandwidth, which in most instances show comparatively poor results.  
Both with \ac{TCP} and \ac{UDP}, the test cases using 80~MHz bandwidth exhibit the highest jitter maxima.  
Out of eight test cases with 80~MHz bandwidth, six show a relatively high maximum jitter.  
Only test case 5-5-80-1 exhibits low maximum values, both for \ac{TCP} and for \ac{UDP}.  

It was initially expected that test cases with lower bandwidth would show poorer performance, since the channel is more heavily utilized.  
This expectation is generally confirmed, as both the average and maximum jitter tend to decrease with increasing bandwidth.  
However, the 80~MHz test cases form an exception to this trend.  

A correlation between the factors standard and frequency in the test cases and the jitter could not be identified.  

Having established the behavior of the test system under ideal conditions, the following section examines the results in a more realistic environment.  

\section{Average disturbance test}  \label{subsec:medium}

\ref{fig:mid_tcp} and \ref{fig:mid_udp} illustrate the results of the test runs in an environment with average interference.
Eine Umgebung mit mittleren Störungen entpricht einem typischen Büro- oder Wohnumfeld.
In diesem existieren andere Netzwerke und elektrische Geräte, die Interferenzen verursachen können.
Es existieren jedoch keien Geräte oder Netzwerke die speziell aufgestellt sind, um das testsystem zu stören.
In den Grafiken \ref{fig:mid_tcp} und \ref{fig:mid_udp} sind die Ergebnisse der Testläufe für den maximalen und durchschnittlichen Jitter dargestellt.
Dieser wird prozentual ins Verhötnis zum Jitter in der Umgebung mit geringen Störungen gesetzt.
Dieser Test wurde bereits in Kapitel \ref{subsec:low} beschrieben.

In den Ergebnissen ist generell zu erkennen, dass die Maxima bie den \ac{TCP} Testfällen stärker angestiegen ist, als die Testfälle mit \ac{UDP}.
Der Mittelwert hingegen ist bei \ac{UDP} stärker angestiegen.
Beide Protokolle zeigen jedoch generell einen höheren Jitter.
Jedoch ist der Jitter im Mittel mit \ac{TCP} gleich geblieben.

Es ist außerdem klar zu erkennen, dass die Testfälle mit \ac{QoS} generell einen geringeren Anstieg des Jitters aufweisen.
Bei beiden Protokollen liegen sowohl im durchschnitt als auch im Maximum alle hohen Ausreißer bei den Testfällen ohne \ac{QoS}.
Insbesondere in den Testfällen auf dem 6 GHz Band ist ein besonders geringer Anstieg des Jitters zu erkennen.
Dies ist vor allem darauf zurückzuführen, dass in diesem Frequenzband zum Zeitpunkt dieser Arbeit nur wenige Geräte betrieben werden und somit kaum Interferenzen vorhanden sind.
Der Wi-Fi Standard 6 zeigt jedoch höhere Anstiege des Jitters, sowohl im Maximum als auch im Durchschnitt, zu den Standarden 4 und 5.
Dabei schneidet der Standard 4 am besten ab.
In Grafik \ref{fig:mid_tcp} kann dies an den Testfällen 6-5-20-0-TCP und 6-5-80-0-TCP besonders gut erkannt werden. 
In Grafik \ref{fig:mid_udp} ist besonders der Testfall 6-2.4-20-0-UDP auffällig.

Es ist zu beobachten, dass im \ac{UDP} Testdurchgang der testfall 6-2.4-20-0-UDP besonders stark von den Störungen betroffen ist und eine 8x höhere maximale Ausprägung des Jitters aufweist, als die durchschnittliche Anstiegsrate im Maximum.
Im \ac{TCP} Testdurchgang ist besonders der Testfall 5-5-40-0-TCP auffällig, welcher eine 7x höhere maximale Ausprägung des Jitters aufweist, als die durchschnittliche Anstiegsrate im Maximum.
Diese Ausreißer geschehen in nahezu allen Testconfigurationen, augenommen davon ist ledigilich der Testfall 6-6-160-*-*.
Jedoch sind die Ausreßger besonders im 2.4 GHz Band mit 20 MHz Bandbreite und in testdurchgägngen mit 80 MHz Bandbreite vertreten, daher repräsentiert der gezeigt Testlauf das allgemeinbild sehr gut.
Des weiteren ist auffällig, dass Ausreißer bei Tsetdurchgängen mit \ac{QoS} deutlich seltender auftreten und des Weiteren eine geringere Ausprägung aufweisen.

Ausreißer treten dabei immer in den Phasen 3 und 6 auf.
In anderen Worten im Link- und Hardware-Layer des Netzwerkstacks.
Dabei ist die Clientseite sowohl im Mittel, als auch in den Maxima stärker betroffen.
Wie in Abbildung \ref{fig:pi_wifi_general} zu sehen müssen beide Geräte in der RT-Verbindung, sowohl Daten senden, als auch Empfangen.
In der Bulk-Datenübertragung hingegen sendet nur der Client-Daten, der Server empfängt nur.
Daraus kann geschlussfolgert werden, dass das Senden von Daten in einer Wi-Fi Verbindung störanfälliger ist, als das Empfangen.

In dieser Arbeit werden fünf Stufen von \ac{QoS} betrachtet.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\ac{QoS} 0} bedeutet, dass kein \ac{QoS} verwendet wird.
    \item \textbf{\ac{QoS} 1} bedeutet, dass der \ac{DSCP} Wert 0xB8 verwendet wird, welcher der \ac{EF} Klasse entspricht und wie im RFC 3246 \cite{rfc3246} beschrieben, extra für latenzkritische Anwendungen vorgesehen ist.
    \item \textbf{\ac{QoS} 2} bedeutet, dass die CS7 Klasse verwendet wird, welche in \cite[p. 27]{rfc4594} für Steuerungsanwendungen empfohlen wird.
          Diese Klasse ist nicht für Nutzeranwendungen vorgesehen, sondern für Netzwerkmanagement und Steuerungsanwendungen.
          Da diese jedoch mit einer höheren Priorität laufen, als Nutzer-Datenverkehr, wird in dieser Arbeit zur vollständigkeit, überprüft, ob diese Klasse Vorteile für eine Echtzeit-Wi-Fi Verbindung bringt.
          Da im Testsystem, welches in dieser ARbeit verwendet wird, lediglich eine Peer-to Peer Verbindung besteht, kann mit dieser Priorisierung kein Netzwerkmanagement gestört werden und daher die Klasse CS7 gefahrlos benutzt werden.
    \item In \textbf{\ac{QoS} 3} wird sowohl der \ac{DSCP} Wert 0xB8, als auch die \ac{qdisc} \ac{HTB} verwendet.  
    \item 
\end{itemize}
